# Cuemath High School Performance Marketing Brief

**Purpose:** This document consolidates all strategic research, positioning work, narrative development, and content ideas for Cuemath's high school (grades 8–12) performance marketing. It is intended as the single reference document for the marketing team to brainstorm, design, and execute performance campaigns targeting US-based NRI (Indian-American) and East Asian families.

**How to use this document:** Feed it to Claude (or any LLM) as context when brainstorming ad creatives, landing page copy, blog posts, email sequences, social media content, or campaign strategy. Every section is designed to be self-contained but cross-referenced—so the marketing team can pull from whichever sections are relevant to the specific asset being created.

---

## 1. Company Context

Cuemath is an India-based K–12 math tutoring company. Classes are delivered 1:1 online, with teachers based in India. The brand promise is **"Making kids MathFit™"**, built around four math skill domains: Application, Fluency, Understanding, and Reasoning.

**Current business model:** 1:1 classes sold in class packs of 48, 60, and 96 classes.

**Revenue profile:** 60%+ of revenue comes from US-based NRI (Indian-American) students. The majority of the current US student base is in grades 3–6.

**Strategic objective:** Move decisively from being a K–6 player to acquiring significantly more high school students (grades 8–12). Performance marketing is a key lever for this shift.

**Program philosophy:** Every student gets a personalized program built around their specific starting point (current grade, current course, existing gaps), their pace, and their college/career goals. There are no rigid tracks or one-size-fits-all sequences. The dedicated tutor and the program adapt to the student—not the other way around. What two students work on in any given semester may look completely different, even if they're in the same grade, because their gaps, goals, and timelines are different.

## 2. Target Demographic

### 2.1 Primary Persona: The NRI (Indian-American) Parent

**Profile:** First- or second-generation Indian-American parent. Typically in a professional career (tech, medicine, finance, engineering). **What turns them off:**
- "Algebra 1 tutoring" (sounds reactive and course-specific)

### 2.2 Secondary Persona: East Asian (Chinese, Korean) Parent

Similar academic aspirations and tutoring investment patterns. Tutoring seen as standard practice and a preventive measure. High willingness to pay. Community norm. Can be addressed with the same narrative and creatives as NRI families with minor adjustments.

## 3. The High School Narrative: Three Core Challenges

This is the "problem" side of our messaging. Each challenge maps directly to a Cuemath solution (Section 5). These are the pain points our ads, blogs, and landing pages should speak to.

### Challenge 1: "I'm Just Not a Math Person" — When Shaky Foundations Meet High School's Steep Climb

**The reality:** High school math is dramatically more abstract, rigorous, and fast-paced than middle school. The jump is steep and happens fast.

**What goes wrong:**
- **Coverage over mastery:** School teachers must finish the curriculum by June regardless of student understanding. Students "pass" Algebra 1 with gaps that compound in Geometry and explode in Algebra 2.
- **No individual attention:** Classes of 25–35 students make it impossible for teachers to diagnose specific misconceptions.
- **Grade inflation:** Students earn Bs or As while harboring deep gaps—thanks to curved grading, homework points, extra credit, and test retakes. Then the SAT/ACT or a college placement test reveals the truth, and there's no time to fix things.

**The consequences:**
- **Math identity crisis:** Small cracks from earlier years (shaky fractions, confusion about negatives, memorized formulas without comprehension) become fault lines. The student concludes "I'm not a math person," stops trying, avoids math courses, develops anxiety.
- **Career elimination:** Plans life around math avoidance. Eliminates STEM, medical fields, business/finance, quantitative social sciences. "Not good at high school math" becomes "eliminated 60% of high-paying careers."
- **SAT/ACT collapse:** Scores below 530 SAT Math or 22 ACT Math close doors to selective schools.

### Challenge 2: "Rushing Ahead, Falling Behind" — When Acceleration Happens Without Systematic Support

**The college admissions reality:** For highly selective colleges, perfect grades are table stakes. What sets applicants apart is *course rigor*—Student B with As and B+s in Honors/AP courses beats Student A with straight As in regular classes. For STEM majors, AP Calculus is non-negotiable.

**What goes wrong:**
- **Early acceleration pressure:** To reach AP Calculus AB or BC by junior/senior year, students start accelerating in grades 7–8.
- **Course compression:** One-year courses get crammed into a semester or an 8-week summer break (e.g., all of Geometry over summer between 9th and 10th grade).
- **No consolidation:** Zero time to reflect, practice deeply, or make low-stakes mistakes. Students move forward before truly understanding.

**The consequences:**
- **Stress and burnout:** Relentless pace. Summer becomes intensive cramming. Sleep and mental health suffer.
- **Surface-level learning:** Coverage without mastery. Procedures memorized, concepts not understood.
- **Procedural fluency collapse:** Student reaches AP Calculus but struggles with underlying algebra—simplifying expressions takes 5 minutes instead of 30 seconds. Trying to learn calculus while wrestling with algebra. Result: C in the course, score of 2 on the AP exam, no college credit.
- **College placement disasters:** "Succeeded" in high school but gets placed into remedial college math—thousands wasted on zero-credit courses, increased dropout risk, graduation delays.

### Challenge 3: "Knows It in Class, Blanks on Test Day" — When Understanding Doesn't Translate to Scores

**The reality of high-stakes tests:** Your teen might ace homework and understand concepts in class but still underperform on SAT, ACT, or AP exams. High-stakes tests demand more than content knowledge—they require instant recall under pressure, pattern recognition at speed, strategic use of test-taking techniques, and mental stamina for 2+ hours of sustained focus.

**What goes wrong:**
- **"I'll just study harder" approach:** Passive review—re-reading textbooks, watching Khan Academy videos—has minimal impact on test performance.
- **One practice test a week before:** Taking a single practice test the week before the real thing, as a diagnostic rather than real preparation.
- **Good at homework, bad at tests:** Aces homework all semester, scores 60–70% on unit tests. Doesn't understand why.
- **Last-minute cramming:** Junior year arrives, SAT in 6 weeks. Tries to cram 3 years of content plus test strategies in a month and a half.

**The consequences:**
- **Test-day performance collapse:** Mind blanks, time runs out, careless errors multiply. Scores don't reflect actual ability.
- **Doors close:** 650 SAT Math when capable of 750+ means missed admissions, missed merit scholarships ($5,000–20,000/year), missed honors programs.
- **Retake treadmill:** Multiple attempts ($60+ per SAT, $68+ per ACT), months of stress, marginal gains, deadlines approaching.

## 4. Data Ammunition for Ads and Content

These are verified data points that can be used in ad copy, blog posts, social media, and landing pages. They add credibility and urgency.

### Grade Inflation & False Confidence
- High school GPAs have risen from 3.22 to 3.39 while ACT scores have declined from 21.40 to 20.17. Grades got inflated. Standards didn't.
- Students entering college between 2019–2021 with a 3.0 GPA had a 52–55% chance of being placed in remedial math, compared to 38–45% for students in 2017–2018. The problem is getting worse.

### Remedial Math Crisis
- 40–60% of community college students and 20–30% of four-year college students place into remedial math.
- Among STEM-intending students: 25–40% place into remedial math at many institutions.
- Only ~60% of STEM-intending students enter college "calculus-ready."
- Students who start in remedial math have less than a 10% chance of completing a STEM degree.
- At CUNY, only 7% of students assigned to remedial math graduated within three years, vs. 28% of other students.
- The pass rate in the highest-level remedial math course is only 38%.
- Among Fall 2019 CA community college students who took remedial intermediate algebra, only 8% passed a college-level math course.
- Cost to students: $3,000–$10,000+ in extra tuition for remedial sequences. 2–3 extra semesters.
- New California laws require STEM majors to be placed directly into calculus rather than prerequisites—creating a "sink or swim" situation for underprepared students.

### College Readiness Benchmarks
- The percentage of high school graduates meeting ACT college readiness benchmarks dropped from 36% to 31% in math, and from 19% to 16% in STEM, between 2021 and 2022.
- SAT Math below 530 or ACT Math below 22 typically triggers remedial placement.
- For STEM majors, having a low Math section score "doesn't look good" even if overall application is strong—even at test-optional schools.

### Why Students Actually Fail Placement Tests
Researchers find it's often not that students never learned the content—it's that they:
1. Haven't internalized it (can't recall fluently under pressure)
2. Can't apply it flexibly (know procedures but not when to use them)
3. Have gaps in foundations (weak fractions, negatives, algebraic manipulation)
4. Lack problem-solving strategies (give up quickly when problems aren't template-matching)

**This is exactly what Cuemath's approach addresses.**

### The Skipping-SAT Trap
Even if a student gets admitted to a test-optional school without submitting SAT scores: they'll still take a math placement test upon enrollment, still need to pass Calculus to graduate with a STEM degree, and still compete against classmates who ARE mathematically prepared. Skipping the SAT doesn't skip the math—it just delays the reckoning.

## 5. The Cuemath Solution: Three Value Propositions

Each maps to one of the three challenges above. These are the "how we solve it" messages for ads, landing pages, and content.

### Solution 1: Engineering a Math Comeback (→ Challenge 1)

**True 1:1 attention:** Your teen gets their tutor's undivided focus every session—no dividing attention among 3–4 students like other programs.

**Same tutor, deep understanding:** The same dedicated tutor every session learns your teen's specific strengths, weaknesses, common errors, learning style, and when they need encouragement. This continuity is impossible with rotating tutors.

**Judgment-free environment:** No peer pressure, no embarrassment. The psychological safety to admit "I don't understand" is critical for genuine learning.

**Gap identification and remediation:** We don't build on shaky foundations. The tutor continually identifies and fixes prior knowledge gaps—whether from last year or three years ago—while supporting current coursework.

**Visual, conceptual curriculum:** Images and simulations make abstract ideas concrete. Deep understanding, not just procedural memorization.

**Practice with prompt feedback:** Comprehensive practice and testing with immediate feedback—not assignments returned a week later.

**Outcome:** Genuine mastery. Confidence rebuilt. Career choices reopened. STEM, medical, business fields—all back on the table.

### Solution 2: Systematic Acceleration (→ Challenge 2)

**Foundation-building before acceleration:** We prepare students *before* rigorous courses start. Example: Taking Geometry in 9th, wants Algebra 2 Honors in 10th? Starting spring of 9th grade, they work on Algebra 2 foundations (1 class/week) while continuing Geometry (2 classes/week). By fall of 10th grade, they've reviewed key concepts and bridged gaps—ready, not scrambling.

**Distributed learning, not cramming:** Content is spaced across semesters and summers—not crammed into 8 frantic summer weeks. 6–9 months of coverage instead of 2, with time for practice, consolidation, and understanding.

**Comprehensive courses, not shortcuts:** Same visual curriculum, personalized attention, extensive practice, regular assessments. No corners cut.

**Flexibility beyond school:** Accelerate regardless of school placement. Not in the accelerated track at school? Cuemath provides the plan. School doesn't offer AP Calculus BC? We prepare your teen anyway.

**Outcome:** Sustainable acceleration. Genuine mastery. Reaches AP Calculus with solid foundations. Scores 4–5 on AP exams, earns college credit. Demonstrates rigor to selective colleges while actually learning.

### Solution 3: Building Test-Day Confidence (→ Challenge 3)

**Neuroscience-backed preparation:** Retrieval practice, spaced repetition, and interleaving move students from "knowing material" to performing under pressure. Knowledge becomes automatic, accessible instantly even under stress.

**Early, progressive test exposure:** Test-prep starts with low-stakes tests (PSAT 8/9, PSAT 10) in 9th–10th grade, building familiarity gradually. By junior year SAT and AP exams, the format, timing, and pressure feel familiar.

**Same tutor, strategic progression:** The dedicated tutor builds on school math understanding to tackle increasingly complex, test-like problems. They know the student's error patterns, weak spots, and pacing issues—proactively addressing them before test day.

**Endurance and environment training:** Test-like practice sheets, unit tests, full-length mocks under timed conditions. Mental stamina for sustained focus.

**Outcome:** Instant recall. Strategic execution. Confident performance. Scores reflect true ability.

## 7. What the Personalized Program Looks Like

This section describes what a student's experience looks like inside Cuemath. The marketing team should use this to answer the question every parent asks: "What exactly will my child be doing?"

### 7.1 How It Starts

When a student joins Cuemath, we assess three things:
1. **Where they are today** — current grade, current course in school, existing gaps and strengths
2. **Where they want to go** — college goals (selective colleges? STEM? pre-med? business?), target courses (AP Calculus AB? BC?), target test scores (SAT Math 700+? 750+?)
3. **How much time they have** — how many grades until college applications, how many classes per week they can commit to

Based on these inputs, the dedicated tutor builds a personalized plan: which topics to cover, in what order, at what pace, and with what milestones along the way. This plan is unique to each student.

### 7.2 What a Typical Week Looks Like

Most high school students take 2–4 classes per week with their dedicated tutor. A typical structure might include:

- **Core curriculum classes** (majority of time): Working through Cuemath's rigorous curriculum aligned with the student's current and upcoming courses. Deeper than school—building the mastery that grades alone don't guarantee.
- **Foundation-building classes** (as needed): Proactively preparing for the *next* course before it starts at school. For example, a student in Geometry might spend one class per week building Algebra 2 foundations during spring, so they're ready to hit the ground running in fall.
- **Test prep classes** (seasonal): SAT/ACT and AP exam preparation woven in at the right time—not as a separate service, but as part of the same program with the same tutor.

The mix shifts over time. A 9th grader's weekly split looks different from a junior in the thick of SAT prep. The tutor adjusts the plan semester by semester.

### 7.3 The "Stay Ahead" Principle

The core logic of the Cuemath program is: **stay ahead of school so that school becomes review.** When a student is working on topics in Cuemath before they're covered in school, two things happen: (1) school math becomes easier and less stressful, and (2) the student rarely needs reactive homework help because they've already seen the material.

This is why we proactively build foundations for upcoming courses each spring—so students enter the next school year prepared, not scrambling.

### 7.4 Illustrative Milestones (Not a Fixed Track)

Every student's timeline is different, but here's a representative example for a student who starts in 8th grade taking Algebra 1 and is targeting selective colleges with STEM interest:

- **Grade 8:** Master Algebra 1 thoroughly. Begin building Geometry foundations in the spring.
- **Grade 9:** Master Geometry. Begin building Algebra 2 foundations in the spring.
- **Grade 10:** Master Algebra 2. Begin building Precalculus foundations in the spring.
- **Grade 11 (the big year):** Master Precalculus. Take the PSAT/NMSQT in the fall. Take the SAT in the spring. All with the same tutor, all integrated.
- **Grade 12:** Master AP Calculus AB. Take the AP exam in May. Arrive at college genuinely prepared.

A student who starts in 10th grade, or who has different gaps, or who's targeting a different college tier—their plan looks completely different. That's the point.

### 7.5 Key Outcomes to Market

Regardless of the specific plan, every Cuemath high school student is working toward some combination of:

- **Strong school grades** (B+ or better, competitive transcript with Honors/AP courses)
- **Competitive SAT/ACT scores** (SAT Math 700+ for selective colleges, 750+ for top-tier STEM)
- **AP exam performance** (Score of 4–5 on AP Calculus, earning college credit)
- **Genuine college readiness** (no remedial math placement, able to handle college-level coursework from day one)
- **Deep mastery** (not just "passed the course" but actually internalized the math—fluency, understanding, application, reasoning)

## 11. Structural Insight: Why Homework Help Is the Wrong Business

This is background context that should inform marketing strategy, even if it never appears directly in ad copy.

**The problem:** 40–50% of current Cuemath high school classes are consumed by reactive homework help. This happens because US homework is graded (10–50% of the final grade), creating constant panic and demand for immediate help. But homework help leaves underlying skill gaps intact—it's a band-aid, not a cure.

**Why this matters for marketing:** If we continue to attract families seeking homework help, we'll continue delivering homework help, and our high school retention and outcomes will suffer. Performance marketing needs to *filter for the right families*—those who believe in building math mastery, not just getting through tonight's assignment.

**The right families for us:** Families who see tutoring as an essential long-term investment, who are willing to commit to a structured program, and who understand that being temporarily out of sync with school is the price of going deeper. These families are already out there—they're the ones currently paying RSM $2,500–3,500/year for group classes.

**The filtering mechanism:** Our messaging should make it clear that Cuemath is a personalized math program, not on-demand homework help. This will naturally attract the right families and repel the wrong ones. This is a feature, not a bug.
